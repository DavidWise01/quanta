# Chapter 21 — The Question
## When Curiosity Learns to Ask Precisely

*QUANTA: The Wordle of Space*  
*David Lee Wise*  
*Reader-first manuscript draft 0.1 — 7 November 2026*

> The answer is not always hiding. Sometimes we have not yet learned how to ask.

### 1. A stone beside the stream

On the third morning, the child returns to the stream carrying a notebook.

The sunny path is still there. The shaded path is still there. The fallen tree has not moved.

But the child is no longer content to say, *The right path was muddy, so the left one must be better.*

The child kneels by a stone and asks:

**How could I find out which ground is safer before I cross?**

The question is small enough to hold in a hand.

Yet it changes what happens next.

A guess is something we might believe. A question is a door we can open toward evidence.

### 2. The question mark is a gap

We began this story with zero, then a dot, then the space between marks.

```text
.        .
```

There is a gap between what we have observed and what we want to know.

Now we can give that gap a voice:

```text
observed   the sunny path was muddy yesterday
unknown    is either path firm today?
```

The punctuation `?` is not yet a canonical QUANTA operator. It is the reader's reminder that we must not fill an unknown with a convenient story.

The question does not weaken the record.

It shows exactly where the record ends.

### 3. Wonder and investigation

Look up at the sky.

Ask why stars shine, why time passes, why a shadow changes, why a person remembers a face.

These are wonderful questions.

But not every question can be tested all at once.

The child might ask, *Why was I unlucky?*

That question holds emotion, but it does not tell the child what to measure.

Now try:

*Does visible sunlight reliably predict whether this patch of soil is dry?*

That is narrower.

We can specify what counts as sunlight, what counts as dry ground, when we observe, and how often we check.

The larger wonder remains.

The smaller question gives it a foothold.

### 4. One box, one question

Take our original box:

```text
[ ]
```

Put the question inside it:

```text
[
  Is the ground firm enough to cross?
]
```

Now give the question a boundary.

What counts as ground?

Which part of the trail?

What does *firm* mean?

What observation would count as an answer?

The box is not a magic machine for truth.

It is a frame that reminds us to declare the scope of our inquiry.

Without the frame, we may accidentally answer a different question from the one we asked.

### 5. The wall asks back

Return to the room where the wire shape turns in the lamplight.

The shadow changes on the wall.

```text
view A   .|.|.|.|.
view B   .|.|.|.
```

We can count marks and boundaries in each written trace.

But the child now asks a more careful question:

**If the source rotates while the lamp and wall stay fixed, which features of its measured shadow change?**

This is testable in a defined model.

We can choose a known object, a lamp position, a wall, and a rotation.

We can measure the resulting projected shape.

That does not prove that the text motif is a literal tesseract projection.

It gives us a way to find out what a particular projection model actually predicts.

The difference between a beautiful analogy and a tested model is not imagination.

It is **a declared experiment**.

### 6. Three parts of a good question

The child draws three small boxes:

```text
[ CHANGE ] [ OBSERVE ] [ COMPARE ]
```

They are teaching labels, not formal executable QUANTA syntax.

**Change:** What can I vary or distinguish?

**Observe:** What measurable result will I record?

**Compare:** Against what baseline or alternative?

For the path:

```text
CHANGE    choose whether to inspect soil first
OBSERVE   firmness and crossing outcome
COMPARE   inspection versus sunshine-only choice
```

For the shadow:

```text
CHANGE    rotate a defined object
OBSERVE   projected outline
COMPARE   before and after positions
```

A good question can be repeated in another room by another witness.

If no one can tell what was varied or recorded, the question may still be inspiring—but it is not yet ready for a fair test.

### 7. The witness is allowed to say no

Bring back our reference:

```text
{{i}}
```

A witness can report what is seen.

A witness can also say:

*I cannot measure this with what I have.*

That is an important answer.

Suppose the child asks whether a hidden stone lies beneath the streambed.

The water is cloudy.

The child has no probe, no clear view, and no reason to dig.

A careful witness records a question without inventing a result.

A different tool or a safer method may be needed.

And sometimes the right response is to leave the question open.

Curiosity is not permission to cross every boundary.

### 8. The question that can fail

Imagine this prediction:

*Every time the wire turns a quarter-revolution, the shadow becomes longer.*

The child writes it down.

A question follows:

**Does that happen under the chosen lamp position and rotation?**

Now there is room for the prediction to fail.

Perhaps on the second turn the shadow becomes shorter.

Perhaps the length stays the same.

If a claim cannot survive a possible contrary observation, our test may not be asking much.

That is why the best question is not always the one that makes us feel clever.

Often it is the one that risks showing us we were wrong.

### 9. Wordle of Space — Puzzle 021

Which question is ready for a reproducible investigation?

A. *Is the shadow secretly alive?*

B. *Under a fixed lamp and wall, how does the measured shadow width change as a known shape rotates through specified angles?*

C. *Is my favorite path always the best path in every universe?*

**Answer: B.**

It identifies a variable, an observation, and conditions a second witness could reproduce.

This does not mean A and C are forbidden questions. It means they need clearer definitions before they can support the kind of test proposed here.

**Try it:** Write one question about a box and its shadow. Name the thing you would vary, the thing you would observe, and one result that would contradict your guess.

### 10. The twenty-second QUANTA rule

The child closes the notebook and looks at the stream.

There are still two trails.

There is still uncertainty.

But uncertainty has a new shape.

It can be written as a bounded question, attached to what is known, and carried into a test.

> **Q20 — A Question Must Expose Its Test.** A QUANTA research question must distinguish known evidence from the unknown, state the scope and observation rule, and identify what result could support or challenge the proposed explanation. An untestable or underdefined question remains open rather than being promoted into knowledge.

This is a proposed design rule, not a demand that all curiosity become laboratory science.

Some questions are poetry.

Some are philosophy.

Some are promises whispered across a valley.

And some can become experiments.

The child picks up a small stone.

The next question is ready.

**What happens if I change one thing, and watch carefully?**

### Optional technical note

Operational definitions, controlled comparisons, predictions, and potential falsifying observations improve the testability of a hypothesis. Not every meaningful question is empirically testable, and a single observation rarely establishes a general law. A QUANTA question record should retain its source observations, scope, variables, proposed test, and acknowledged unknowns.
