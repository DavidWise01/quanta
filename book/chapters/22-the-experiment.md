# Chapter 22 — The Experiment
## When a Question Meets a Controlled Change

*QUANTA: The Wordle of Space*  
*David Lee Wise*  
*Reader-first manuscript draft 0.1 — 7 November 2026*

> The experiment does not ask the world to agree. It makes a place where disagreement can be seen.

### 1. A lamp, a card, and a wall

The child returns to the little room, carrying the notebook from the stream.

The wire shape still hangs before the lamp. Its shadow still wanders across the wall.

But today the child brings something simpler: a square card.

They stand it upright between the light and the wall.

Its shadow looks broad.

Then the child turns the card a little.

The shadow narrows.

A little farther.

Narrower still.

The child smiles.

But then stops.

Did the card's turn cause the change? Or did the lamp move? Was the wall the same distance away? Did the child's own shadow cross the beam?

Yesterday, a question was enough to begin.

Today, the child wants a way to answer it fairly.

### 2. Make one thing move

The notebook opens to a clean page.

```text
CHANGE       card angle
KEEP         lamp position
KEEP         wall position
KEEP         card size
OBSERVE      shadow width
```

The child marks the lamp's place on the table.

Marks the card's center.

Marks the measuring line on the wall.

Then turns only the card.

This is the heart of a controlled comparison: we try to isolate one change while holding other relevant conditions stable.

We can never assume every influence is perfectly controlled. We can name the conditions we chose and watch for deviations.

A good experiment is not a claim of perfect control.

It is an honest attempt to make the cause of a measured difference easier to investigate.

### 3. The first measurement

The child sets the card facing the lamp.

The shadow is broad.

```text
trial 001
card orientation    face-on
shadow width        broad
```

Then turns it partway.

```text
trial 002
card orientation    partly turned
shadow width        narrower
```

Then toward its edge.

```text
trial 003
card orientation    near edge-on
shadow width        very narrow
```

These are qualitative observations for the story, not collected experimental measurements.

A real notebook would specify the angle, width, units, instruments, and uncertainty.

The child does not need a complicated equation to understand the design.

One thing is changed.

One thing is observed.

The result is written down.

### 4. The box and its shadow

We began QUANTA with one box:

```text
[ ]
```

Today it becomes a frame for the experiment.

```text
[
  lamp  →  turning card  →  wall
]
```

The arrows describe an illustrative light path, not a complete optics simulation.

Our older motif returns:

```text
.|.|.|.|.
```

Five marks, four divisions.

We can imagine it as a line on the wall, a sequence of samples, or a story about where the shadow was read.

But a string of marks is not, by itself, the mathematical projection of a tesseract.

To test an actual tesseract projection, we would need a defined four-dimensional object, a projection map, and a measurable output.

The child is testing a physical card's shadow.

The metaphor remains an invitation, not a substitute for the experiment.

### 5. The notebook is an instrument, too

The child records the setup *before* looking for the result.

```text
QUESTION
Will turning the square card change
its apparent shadow width?

PREDICTION
The measured width will decrease
as the card approaches edge-on.

METHOD
Keep lamp and wall positions fixed.
Turn the card through chosen angles.
Measure the shadow at each angle.
```

Writing this first matters.

If the child waits until afterward, it becomes too easy to invent a prediction that fits the measurements.

The page is a small defense against hindsight.

It doesn't guarantee honesty.

It makes the original expectation inspectable.

### 6. When two things move

Now imagine the child accidentally steps against the lamp.

The lamp shifts.

The shadow grows larger.

Was it because of the card's orientation?

We no longer know from that trial alone.

The record should say:

```text
trial 004
card turned
lamp shifted
result uncertain
```

Do not squeeze this measurement into the clean series.

Do not quietly correct it until it agrees.

Mark it as a changed condition.

This is not a wasted page.

It is evidence about the limits of the experiment.

The boundary between a valid comparison and a confounded one is worth preserving.

### 7. Let the child repeat it

The child restores the lamp to its marked position.

Turns the card to the original orientation.

Measures again.

Then repeats the sequence.

Perhaps the widths are close to the first run.

Perhaps there are small differences because the card wobbles or the measurement was imprecise.

A single result can be surprising.

Repeated measurements show whether the result is stable under the declared procedure.

A trustworthy record includes the variation, not only the neatest number.

And if the experiment fails to reproduce, the notebook has discovered another question.

### 8. What about the tesseract?

A friend enters and sees the card's changing shadow.

The child says, “Imagine a four-dimensional shape doing something similar on a three-dimensional wall.”

The friend asks, “Could we test that drawing?”

Yes—but first we must say exactly what we mean by projection.

We could write a mathematical model of a tesseract, select a viewing transformation, and calculate lower-dimensional images.

Then we could compare the predicted outputs with the renderer's actual outputs under known settings.

That would test the model or implementation.

It would not establish that a physical tesseract lives inside the room.

The real pleasure is that QUANTA can keep the metaphor and the test **side by side without confusing them**.

```text
[()]          held-cycle metaphor
.|.|.|.|.     wall-trace motif
{{i}}         witness reference
```

The same little marks can invite imagination and demand precision.

### 9. Wordle of Space — Puzzle 022

The child wants to know whether rotating a card changes its shadow width.

Which experiment gives the clearest comparison?

A. Move the lamp, resize the card, rotate it, and measure only the final shadow.

B. Keep the lamp, wall, and card size fixed; change the card's angle; measure shadow width after each change.

C. Draw a narrower shadow from memory and announce that rotation caused it.

**Answer: B.**

It isolates the intended variable while recording what is observed.

Now suppose the lamp moves during one trial.

Should the child silently discard that detail?

No.

Record the departure from the method. Repeat that trial if possible, and preserve the original account.

**Try it:** Use a card and a desk lamp, with adult help where appropriate. Keep the lamp stable, rotate the card, and write down what you see. Avoid hot light fixtures, and never look directly into bright sources.

### 10. The twenty-third QUANTA rule

The child closes the notebook for a moment.

The lamp is still shining.

The card has turned.

The wall has answered with a different shadow.

And unlike yesterday, the child can say which condition was deliberately varied.

> **Q21 — Experiment With Declared Controls.** A QUANTA experiment record must identify the question, prediction, variable deliberately changed, relevant conditions held fixed, measurement rule, and observed result. Deviations and uncertainty remain part of the record rather than being silently repaired.

The question gave curiosity a boundary.

The experiment gives the boundary something to meet.

```text
[ question ]
     |
[ controlled change ]
     |
[ observation ]
     |
[ comparison ]
```

And still, the world is free to surprise us.

**The experiment did not create the answer. It made the answer easier to witness.**

### Optional technical note

A controlled experiment attempts to isolate the effect of a variable while documenting relevant fixed conditions and confounders. A flat card's apparent projected width under idealized directional light varies with its orientation; real shadows depend on source geometry, distance, blur, and measurement conventions. The chapter reports a conceptual demonstration, not actual measurements. A tesseract projection is a separate mathematical model requiring a specified mapping and rendering procedure.
