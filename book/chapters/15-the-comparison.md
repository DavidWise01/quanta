# Chapter 15 — The Comparison
## When Memory Learns to Notice Difference

*QUANTA: The Wordle of Space*  
*David Lee Wise*  
*Reader-first manuscript draft 0.1 — 7 November 2026*

> A difference is not a mistake. It is a question the records have learned to ask.

### 1. Two pages, one wall

Morning returns to the little room.

The lamp is quiet. The wire shape hangs where it did last night, and the wall is waiting.

The child opens the notebook.

```text
001    short shadow
002    long shadow
003    unknown
004    short shadow
```

The child looks at the first and fourth entries.

Both say *short*.

Are they the same observation?

No. They are two different records, made at different moments. They describe similar appearances.

Then the child looks at entries 001 and 002.

One says short. One says long.

What changed?

Perhaps the shape turned. Perhaps the lamp moved. Perhaps the observer stood elsewhere. Perhaps the scale used to describe the shadow changed.

The notebook has discovered a difference.

But it has not yet discovered its cause.

### 2. Place two records beside each other

This is the smallest comparison:

```text
[ 001 : short ]    [ 002 : long ]
```

We do not need to erase either entry.

We do not need to invent a third observation.

We only need to choose a way to read the two together.

For this chapter, let the vertical mark remind us of a distinction:

```text
[ short ] | [ long ]
```

The bar is not yet a complete comparison operator. It simply helps us see that the two held descriptions have been placed side by side.

The difference lies in what the entries say.

To compare meaningfully, we must know *which property* we are comparing.

### 3. Comparing the same thing

Imagine two children measuring the same shadow.

One uses a ruler marked in centimeters.

The other uses a ruler marked in inches.

They return with different numbers.

Does the shadow have two different lengths?

Not necessarily.

They may have described the same length in different units.

Or perhaps they measured at different times, or from different edges.

So the child adds a small note beside each record:

```text
001  shadow length  |  short  |  visual estimate
002  shadow length  |  long   |  visual estimate
```

At least now the descriptions use the same named property and the same kind of rough estimate.

That does not make the measurement perfect.

It makes the comparison clearer.

**A difference only becomes useful when we know what is being compared.**

### 4. The gap returns

Remember Chapter 3?

Two marks invited a question about what lay between them.

```text
.       .
```

The gap was more than a blank in the drawing.

Now two *records* invite a different kind of gap:

```text
[ before ]       [ after ]
```

We could call this a difference in appearance, a difference in time, or a difference in state.

Those are not automatically the same.

A change in time does not guarantee a change in appearance.

A change in appearance does not guarantee a change in the underlying object.

We have learned enough QUANTA to resist that shortcut.

### 5. The box keeps both stories

Suppose the child has two pages:

```text
[ 001 : short ]
[ 002 : long  ]
```

Now the child wants to add a comparison.

The tempting method is to write the conclusion over the original pages.

But Chapter 14 taught us better.

Instead, add a new record:

```text
003 : compare(001,002)
      property = apparent length
      result   = different descriptions
```

Here `compare(001,002)` is an explanatory label, not a final QUANTA function.

The earlier entries remain untouched.

The new entry says what was compared and what conclusion the available evidence supports.

Now a third reader can check the conclusion without depending only on the child's memory.

### 6. A shadow can return, but a measurement may not

The wire turns through another cycle.

The shadow appears short again.

```text
001  short
002  long
003  unknown
004  short
```

There are two short reports.

Does that prove the wire returned to exactly the same physical orientation?

No.

Different orientations can sometimes produce similar projections. Different three-dimensional structures can even have indistinguishable shadows under a particular viewing arrangement.

A tesseract is richer still: a lower-dimensional projection generally cannot, by appearance alone, disclose every feature of the original higher-dimensional object.

The wall offers evidence.

The witness offers a report.

The record preserves that report.

Comparison tells us what appears equal or different **under the chosen reading**.

It cannot manufacture information the projection never contained.

### 7. Equality is a choice of question

Consider:

```text
A: [ . ]
B: [ . ]
```

The two drawings match exactly as written.

But we may ask different questions:

Are their characters identical? Yes.

Do they contain one visible dot each? Yes.

Are they records of the same physical object? We cannot tell.

Did they occur at the same time? We cannot tell.

Are they produced by the same witness? We cannot tell.

Each question uses a different comparison criterion.

This is why QUANTA must distinguish **same appearance**, **same identity**, and **same state**.

Those phrases may agree in a particular model.

They are not interchangeable by default.

### 8. Comparison without control

Imagine that the two witnesses from Chapter 13 disagree.

One reports a long shadow.

The other reports a short shadow.

A careless system might decide that only one account may survive, erase the other, and declare victory.

But what if the observers were standing on opposite sides of the wall?

Their reports may differ because their viewing conditions differ.

A useful comparison preserves both accounts:

```text
W1 : long  |  angle A
W2 : short |  angle B
```

Then it asks what can explain the difference.

Perhaps one observer was mistaken.

Perhaps both were accurate about different views.

Comparison is not a command that the evidence must agree.

It is an invitation to find out where the relation changes.

### 9. Wordle of Space — Puzzle 015

The notebook contains:

```text
001  [ . ]  at morning
002  [ . ]  at evening
```

Which conclusion is justified from the written forms alone?

A. The same physical dot remained inside the same box all day.

B. The two drawn forms have the same visible bracket-and-dot pattern.

C. The shadow never moved.

**Answer: B.**

The written pattern matches. Identity, motion, and continuity require additional observations and rules.

Now compare:

```text
003  [ . ]
004  [ . . ]
```

What is a safe first observation?

The second drawing contains one more dot mark than the first. That is a difference in the **representation**.

What caused the difference is still a question.

**Try it:** Make two sketches of the same object from different angles. Write one comparison about the drawings and a separate hypothesis about what happened to the object.

### 10. The sixteenth QUANTA rule

We have carried the story through a long journey.

Zero gave us a beginning.

The dot gave us a place.

The gap gave us a relationship.

The boundary and box gave us distinctions and containment.

The loop gave us return.

The vessel, path, signal, code, and grammar gave us ways to describe passage and meaning.

The transformation gave us change.

The witness gave us observation.

The record gave us memory.

Now comparison gives memory a way to ask what is different.

> **Q14 — Compare Without Conflation.** A QUANTA comparison must identify its source records, the property and reading rule being compared, and the scope of the result. Similarity of appearance must not silently become a claim of identical identity, state, or cause.

The child closes the notebook.

On the wall, the shadow has become short again.

The child no longer asks only whether the shadow looks familiar.

The child asks:

**Familiar in what way?**

And that small question changes everything.

Because a language that can remember and compare without rewriting the evidence has begun to learn.

### Optional technical note

String equality, structural equality, equality of measured quantities, state equivalence, and persistent-object identity are distinct equivalence criteria. Comparison results depend on selected fields, measurement scales, and stated assumptions. The examples use prose labels as teaching scaffolding; QUANTA comparison semantics remain to be formalized. A projection is generally an information-losing map, and equality of projected images need not imply equality of source objects.
