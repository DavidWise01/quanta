# Chapter 16 — The Difference
## When Comparison Reveals What Changed

*QUANTA: The Wordle of Space*  
*David Lee Wise*  
*Reader-first manuscript draft 0.1 — 7 November 2026*

> A difference is a trace between two descriptions. It tells us what changed on the page before it tells us why.

### 1. The small missing star

A child returns to the room with the lamp, the turning shape, and the shadow on the wall.

The notebook lies open at two drawings:

```text
BEFORE        AFTER

[ . ]         [ . . ]
```

The child does not need a theory of the universe to notice what happened on the page.

One drawing has one dot.

The other has two.

Something is different.

But the child has learned enough to ask a second question.

Did a new object appear inside the box? Was the first dot copied? Did the observer change how the marks were drawn? Was the drawing merely corrected?

The pictures do not say.

The first gift of difference is **description**.

The second is **a reason to investigate**.

### 2. The mark between the marks

We already have a boundary:

```text
|
```

And we have a habit of placing two records beside it:

```text
[ . ] | [ . . ]
```

The vertical line is a separator in this sketch, not yet a complete arithmetic operator.

Now add an ordinary explanatory word:

```text
before : [ . ]
after  : [ . . ]

change : one additional dot mark
```

We did not erase the original records.

We did not pretend to know whether the dot was born, copied, carried, or discovered.

We named the **difference in the representation**.

That small discipline lets a language grow without losing itself.

### 3. The same form in a different place

Now compare:

```text
BEFORE        AFTER

[ . ]         [ . ]
```

The characters match.

What if the second drawing comes from another box across the room?

What if the observer moved?

What if someone copied the first picture and pasted it into another page?

We can truthfully say the visible forms are identical. We cannot conclude the entire world remained unchanged.

This is why the record needs more than a sketch when identity, time, or location matters.

```text
form     [ . ]
source   box A
order    001
```

And later:

```text
form     [ . ]
source   box B
order    002
```

Now the drawings match, but the source labels differ.

Difference can live in the *context*, not only in the ink.

### 4. A difference has a direction

Consider:

```text
A: [ . ]
B: [ . . ]
```

Reading from A to B, we say one mark was added.

Reading from B to A, we say one mark was removed.

The pair of pictures is the same.

The direction of comparison changes the description of the edit.

That is why *before* and *after* are not decorative labels.

```text
A → B : add .
B → A : remove .
```

Here `add` and `remove` describe the written strings. They do not prove that a physical object entered or left a chamber.

A language that records direction can tell a more precise story about its own representations.

### 5. The loop that comes back different

Return to our engine:

```text
[()]
```

A cycle turns. A piston moves. A wheel completes a revolution.

We learned in Chapter 6 that returning to a starting position does not erase the journey.

Now imagine observing the chamber before and after one complete cycle:

```text
BEFORE          AFTER

[ ( . ) ]       [ ( . . ) ]
```

The containing structure is drawn the same way.

The marked interior differs.

Perhaps our model intends to represent a changing state of the chamber. Perhaps the symbol is only an illustrative notation. We cannot assume a mechanism from the drawings alone.

But the comparison can tell us something definite:

**The outer form is preserved in the representation; the inner dot count changed.**

This is a useful kind of difference.

It separates what remained stable from what did not.

### 6. The shadow on the wall

Now return to the tesseract-shadow image that carried us through Chapter 12.

One box.

One wall.

One changing projection.

```text
view 001 : .|.|.|.|.
view 002 : .|.|.|.
```

The strings have different numbers of dot marks and separators.

We may count those differences exactly.

But we cannot automatically say a four-dimensional object lost a face, changed dimension, or passed through a physical boundary. That would require an explicit mapping between the higher-dimensional object and its observed projection.

What we can say is beautiful enough:

**The wall received two different traces.**

We can preserve both.

We can name the changed features.

We can ask what transformations might produce them.

And we can leave the answer open until evidence arrives.

### 7. The honest difference ledger

The child begins keeping a new page.

Not a replacement for the witness notebook.

A companion to it.

```text
D001
from     record 001
to       record 002
criterion written dot count
result   +1 dot
cause    unknown
```

The notation is explanatory, not yet canonical QUANTA grammar.

A difference entry carries the two source references, the chosen comparison criterion, the observed result, and what remains unknown.

Later, a witness may discover that the second drawing was a copy.

That new observation can be appended, referencing D001.

The original difference does not need to be falsified or erased.

It was true as a comparison of the *written forms*.

What changes is our understanding of how those forms came to exist.

### 8. Not every difference is an error

A seed grows into a tree.

The tree is not a failed seed.

A melody changes notes.

The second note is not a broken first note.

A child learns a new word.

The new word is not proof that the old one was wrong.

A difference may reflect growth, movement, correction, variation, or noise.

To call it an improvement, we need a **goal and a criterion**.

To call it a failure, we need an **expectation and a test**.

The comparison itself is not a judge.

It is an instrument for seeing what changed.

### 9. Wordle of Space — Puzzle 016

Two records show:

```text
001   [ . ]
002   [ . . ]
```

Which statement is justified?

A. A new physical particle was created.

B. The second written form contains one additional dot mark.

C. The box grew larger in physical space.

**Answer: B.**

The representation changed in a specific, countable way. Its physical cause remains unknown.

Now reverse the reading order.

```text
002 → 001
```

What changes?

The edit description reverses from *one dot added* to *one dot removed*. The underlying pair of records remains intact.

**Try it:** Draw two nested QUANTA boxes. Change only the innermost mark. Write one sentence about what stayed the same and another about what changed.

### 10. The seventeenth QUANTA rule

The record can remember.

The comparison can distinguish.

Now the difference can describe a change without claiming more than the evidence supports.

> **Q15 — Difference With Scope.** A QUANTA difference report must name the two source states or records, the direction and criterion of comparison, and the exact representational change found. It must not silently promote that change into an explanation of physical cause, identity, value, or intent.

Look at our little pair once more:

```text
[ . ] → [ . . ]
```

Something changed on the page.

We know precisely what.

We do not yet know why.

And that is not an embarrassment.

It is the space where the next discovery can happen.

The child closes the notebook and looks toward the wall.

The shadow has shifted again.

This time, rather than guessing what the shape must be, the child writes:

**I know what is different. I am still learning what made it so.**

### Optional technical note

A textual diff reports changes under a chosen representation and alignment policy. It does not establish the causal mechanism behind those changes. Even an apparently minimal edit may be one among multiple equivalent edit descriptions, so a rigorous implementation must specify its comparison algorithm and tie-breaking conventions. Physical inferences about projections require a separately defined model.
